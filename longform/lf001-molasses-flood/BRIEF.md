# LF001 — The Great Molasses Flood (video largo #1)

## Por qué este tema
- **Demanda probada:** historia "bizarra pero real y conocida", con búsquedas todos los años y canales grandes que ya la cubrieron. Eso prueba interés, no saturación: lo que nos diferencia es la **reconstrucción forense en 3D, segundo a segundo, con rayos X** (nadie lo mostró así).
- **Todo lo que retiene:** espectáculo (ola de 7,6 m), villano (el tesorero que no consultó a un ingeniero), encubrimiento (pintaron el tanque de marrón), giro científico (el frío fue lo que mató) y juicio con final.
- **Encaja con la marca:** el corte en rayos X del tanque y de la ola es el formato del canal llevado a largo.
- **Embudo listo:** el Short `xr004-molasses-flood` ya está en cola; pasa a ser el tráiler (video relacionado = este largo).
- Datos de los Shorts (XR-06, 18/11): solo 3 Shorts con métricas (~1.200 vistas cada uno, 49–65 % de retención inicial); los de ElevenLabs todavía no salieron. **No alcanzan para elegir el tema por datos**: por eso se eligió por demanda externa. Desde el video #3 el tema sale de los Shorts que mejor retengan.

## GUION (v1, ≈ 1.260 palabras)

### COLD OPEN
This is a wave of molasses.
Twenty-five feet tall. A hundred and sixty feet wide. Moving at thirty-five miles an hour.
Through a city.
On January fifteenth, nineteen nineteen, it tore through Boston and killed twenty-one people.
And the strangest part isn't the wave.
It's that the company saw it coming.
For three years, that tank leaked. Kids from the neighborhood filled buckets from the drips.
And instead of fixing it... the company painted it brown.
Today, we X-ray the Great Molasses Flood. Second by second. Rivet by rivet.
And by the end, you'll understand why molasses, of all things, turned out to be the perfect trap.

### CHAPTER 1 — THE TANK
To understand this disaster, you have to understand why there was a lake of molasses sitting next to the Boston waterfront in the first place.
It's 1915. War is tearing Europe apart, and war runs on explosives.
Explosives need industrial alcohol. And industrial alcohol can be made by fermenting molasses.
So a company called United States Industrial Alcohol decides it needs a giant storage tank in Boston.
Right in the North End. One of the most crowded neighborhoods in America.
The man in charge isn't an engineer.
His name is Arthur Jell, and he's the company's treasurer.
He has a deadline: finish the tank by the end of 1915, in time for a ship arriving from Cuba. Hit it, and a promotion is waiting.
So the tank goes up fast. Fifty feet tall. Ninety feet across.
Big enough to hold more than two million gallons.
And here's where it gets dark.
Before you fill a tank like this, there's a basic test: fill it with water, and look for leaks.
Jell skipped it.
Later, he admitted he never even consulted an engineer.
The first time the tank was filled, it started to leak.
Not a little. Brown streaks ran down the seams. People living nearby heard it groan when it was filled.
Neighborhood kids figured out that if you held a cup under the drips, you got free molasses.
So what did the company do?
They didn't empty it. They didn't reinforce it.
They painted the tank brown.
Same color as the leaks.
Problem solved.

### CHAPTER 2 — THE WARM DAY
Now fast-forward three years.
January, nineteen nineteen. The war is over, but the tank is still there, still leaking, still brown.
A few days before the disaster, a ship arrives from Cuba and pumps in a fresh load of molasses. Roughly six hundred thousand gallons.
That new molasses was warm. And it went into a tank full of older, colder molasses.
Then the weather did something strange.
After a brutally cold spell, the temperature jumped. By the morning of January fifteenth, it felt almost like spring.
Some researchers think the molasses inside may have been fermenting, releasing gas, and adding even more pressure on the steel.
Whatever the exact trigger, the tank was now holding about two point three million gallons.
Molasses is about forty percent heavier than water.
That's around twelve thousand tons of syrup, pushing outward against walls that were never tested.
And just outside those walls, it's lunchtime in one of the busiest corners of Boston.
Workers on break. Wagons on the street. A firehouse right next door.
An elevated train rattling overhead.
Nobody's looking at the tank.
Why would they? It's been standing there for three years.

### CHAPTER 3 — 12:30 PM
At around twelve-thirty, it starts.
Survivors described a sound like a machine gun.
That was the rivets. The steel pins holding the tank together, shooting out one after another.
Then the steel itself splits open.
And two point three million gallons come out all at once.
Here's the thing people get wrong about this story.
They imagine a slow, sticky ooze.
That is not what happened.
Twelve thousand tons of liquid, dropping from fifty feet up, turns into a wave.
Up to twenty-five feet tall. Wide enough to cover the whole street. Moving at thirty-five miles an hour.
You cannot outrun that. Nobody could.
The wave hits the firehouse and shoves it off its foundation.
It slams into the supports of the elevated railway.
It crushes buildings, flips wagons, and drags everything in its path toward the harbor.
A newspaper at the time wrote a line that's hard to forget.
"Horses died like so many flies on sticky fly-paper."
In just a few minutes, it was over.
Twenty-one people were dead. Around a hundred and fifty were injured.
And here's what makes it even worse.
The wave wasn't the only thing that killed them.

### CHAPTER 4 — WHY MOLASSES
In twenty sixteen, a team of scientists at Harvard decided to finally figure out what happened that day.
They went through old maps, weather records, and hundreds of pages of historical accounts.
They even tested corn syrup inside a walk-in refrigerator, to see how a thick, sugary liquid behaves in the cold.
And they found the missing piece.
Remember, that molasses had just arrived warm.
When it hit the freezing January air, it started cooling fast.
And when molasses cools, it doesn't just slow down.
It gets thicker. And thicker.
Imagine being knocked down by that wave and surviving it.
Now you're stuck in something that's slowly turning into glue.
The more you struggle, the more it holds you.
Rescuers reached people, but pulling them free was brutally hard.
The researchers concluded that the cold made everything worse. Rescue was harder. Recovery was slower.
On a warmer day, the same flood would likely have been much less deadly.
There's an old saying: slow as molasses in January.
On January fifteenth, nineteen nineteen, it was the opposite. Fast enough to kill. And then, slow enough to trap.

### CHAPTER 5 — THE COVER STORY
After a disaster like this, you'd expect the company to take responsibility.
Instead, it blamed someone else.
United States Industrial Alcohol said the tank didn't fail.
It said anarchists had blown it up.
It wasn't a random excuse. Anarchist bombings really were in the news that year, and blaming them was a convenient story.
The survivors and families sued.
More than a hundred lawsuits were combined into one massive case.
And the court handed it to one man: an auditor named Hugh Ogden.
He took the job seriously.
Over almost six years, he heard from around three thousand witnesses.
The testimony filled roughly forty-five thousand pages.
Explosives experts. Engineers. Workers. Survivors.
And in April of nineteen twenty-five, he gave his answer.
There was no bomb.
The tank was simply too weak for what it was holding.
The company was responsible.
The families received roughly six hundred twenty-eight thousand dollars, split among all of them.

### CHAPTER 6 — THE FIX
So what was actually wrong with the tank?
Workers later testified that its steel walls were thinner than designed.
And modern analysis found the steel was also too brittle for the load it carried.
Thin. Brittle. Never tested. Leaking for three years.
And the fix was a can of brown paint.
The disaster did change something.
After the flood, Boston started requiring engineers to show their calculations before giant structures like this could go up.
A simple rule.
Have someone who actually knows the math sign off on it.
For decades, people in the North End said that on hot summer days, the streets still smelled sweet.
Today, the spot is a park by the water, with a small plaque.
Most people walk right past it.
But next time someone tells you something is slow as molasses...
remember Boston.
Because on one warm day in January, it wasn't slow at all.
