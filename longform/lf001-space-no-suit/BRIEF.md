# LF001 — Space Without a Suit (video largo #1)

Tema elegido por la dueña el 18/11/2026 (descartó la inundación de melaza de Boston).

## Por qué funciona
- **Búsqueda permanente:** "what happens in space without a suit" se busca todos los años y lo ve gente de los países que más pagan.
- **Formato que retiene:** cuenta regresiva segundo a segundo; el espectador se queda para saber "qué pasa en el segundo siguiente".
- **Casos reales:** Jim LeBlanc (1966, sobrevivió y contó que sintió hervir la saliva) y Soyuz 11 (1971, los únicos 3 humanos muertos en el espacio).
- **Desmiente mitos** (no explotás, no te congelás al instante, la sangre no hierve): cada mito desmentido es un mini-gancho.
- **Rayos X puro:** pulmones, sangre, cerebro y corazón por dentro, segundo a segundo.
- **Embudo:** el Short `xr001-space-no-suit` (ya publicado, ~1.250 vistas) pasa a ser el tráiler: video relacionado = este largo.

## GUION (v2, ≈ 1.820 palabras, ~12,5–13 min)

### COLD OPEN
You have fifteen seconds.
That's how long you stay awake if you're thrown into space without a suit.
Forget the movies. You won't explode. You won't freeze into a statue.
What actually happens is stranger. And we know, because it has happened to real people.
One man felt the saliva boil on his tongue.
Another watched his own hand swell to twice its size, and kept climbing.
The first man to ever walk in space got stuck outside his own spaceship.
One astronaut almost drowned... in space.
And three men never made it home at all.
Today, we X-ray all of it. What happens to your body without a suit, second by second.
Starting with the mistake that would kill you before second one is even over.

### SECOND ZERO
Picture it. You're standing in an airlock. No suit. The outer door starts to open.
Every instinct you have screams the same thing: hold your breath.
Don't.
Right now your lungs are full of air at normal pressure.
Outside, there's almost nothing pushing back.
So that air wants out. Fast.
If you clamp your mouth shut, it expands inside you, and your lungs can tear.
So the first rule of space without a suit is the opposite of what you'd do underwater.
Breathe out. Let it go.
Congratulations. You survived second zero.
Now the strange part starts.

### SECONDS 1 TO 10 — THE BOILING
Here's something most people never think about.
Water doesn't have one boiling point.
On a mountain, water boils at a lower temperature than at the beach, because there's less air pressing down on it.
Go high enough, and the boiling point drops all the way down to thirty-seven degrees Celsius.
Ninety-eight point six Fahrenheit.
Your body temperature.
That height has a name: the Armstrong line. About sixty-three thousand feet up. Almost twice as high as a passenger jet flies.
Which means you don't even need to reach space for this to happen.
Above that line, the moisture on your tongue starts to bubble. Your tears start to boil away.
Scientists call it ebullism. Bubbles forming in your body's fluids because the pressure around you disappeared.
And it doesn't stop at the surface. Water in your tissues starts turning into vapor, and your body begins to swell.
We know exactly what that looks like. Because in nineteen sixty, a man rode straight up through that line... on purpose.
August sixteenth, nineteen sixty. Air Force captain Joe Kittinger is sitting in an open gondola, hanging under a giant balloon, rising over New Mexico.
His suit is pressurized. All of it, except one glove.
The right glove has failed.
As he climbs, his right hand starts to swell. And swell. Until it's about twice its normal size, and so painful he can barely use it.
He doesn't tell anyone. He's afraid they'll call off the mission.
At a hundred and two thousand eight hundred feet, he steps out of the gondola and falls for four and a half minutes.
And three days later, his hand was back to normal.
So, to the big question: if your whole body swells like that... do you pop?
No.
Your skin is tougher than it looks. It stretches, and it holds you together.
And your blood? It doesn't boil either. Your blood vessels keep it under enough pressure to stay liquid.
What about the cold? Space is freezing, right?
Here's the twist. Space is cold, but it's also empty. There's no air to steal your heat quickly.
The fastest cooling happens where water is evaporating. Your nose. Your mouth. Those get close to freezing.
So far, you're swollen, your tongue is fizzing, and your face is going numb.
But none of that is what knocks you out.

### SECONDS 10 TO 15 — LIGHTS OUT
Remember the air you breathed out?
Now there's nothing left in your lungs.
And here's the cruel part. Normally, your lungs load oxygen into your blood.
In a vacuum, it runs the other way. The low pressure pulls oxygen out of your blood.
That oxygen-poor blood leaves your lungs, goes through your heart, and heads for your brain.
The trip takes around nine to twelve seconds.
When it arrives, your brain runs out of fuel.
At roughly fourteen seconds, you're unconscious.
We know that number because of one man.
December fourteenth, nineteen sixty-six. Houston, Texas.
A NASA engineer named Jim LeBlanc is testing a spacesuit prototype inside a giant vacuum chamber.
Mid-test, the hose feeding air into his suit comes loose.
In less than ten seconds, the pressure in his suit crashes to almost nothing. The equivalent of a hundred and twenty thousand feet up.
He stays conscious for about fourteen seconds.
And his last memory before blacking out?
The saliva on his tongue, starting to bubble.
The team brings the pressure back. And Jim LeBlanc wakes up.
He survived, with no lasting damage.
Which raises the real question.
How long do you actually have?

### SECONDS 15 TO 90 — THE CLOCK NOBODY SEES
You're unconscious now. But you're not dead.
Your body goes through a sequence: paralysis, then convulsions, then paralysis again.
It looks terrible. But if pressure comes back in time, it reverses.
So how much time is "in time"?
In the nineteen fifties and sixties, researchers tested this on animals. Experiments nobody would repeat today.
Dogs exposed to near-vacuum for ninety seconds still had a heartbeat. Barely. Around ten beats a minute.
Past two minutes, most didn't make it.
Some chimpanzees lasted longer. A few recovered after three and a half minutes.
For humans, the conclusion was this:
Get someone back to even a quarter of normal pressure within sixty to ninety seconds, and they'll usually survive and recover.
Astronaut Chris Hadfield puts it simply. Under about thirty seconds, with luck, you could walk away fine. Past ninety seconds, it's probably fatal.
Fifteen seconds to act. Ninety for someone to save you.
So the answer is obvious, right? Wear a suit.
Except the very first man to step into space found out that the suit can try to kill you too.

### THE SUIT THAT TRIED TO KILL HIM
March eighteenth, nineteen sixty-five.
Soviet cosmonaut Alexei Leonov crawls through a fabric airlock, inflated like a tent on the side of his capsule.
And he lets go.
For the first time in history, a human being is floating free in space.
He later said it was so quiet, he could hear his own heart beating.
But within minutes, something is wrong.
His suit is inflating.
Inside, it's pressurized. Outside, there's nothing. So the suit puffs up like a balloon and goes stiff.
His fingers pull away from the tips of his gloves. He can't even reach the camera switch on his own leg.
And when he gets back to the airlock, he has a much bigger problem.
He doesn't fit.
He's outside the spacecraft, in a suit that's too big to get back in, with the clock running.
So Leonov makes a decision on his own. Without telling anyone on the ground.
He opens a valve and starts letting air out of his suit.
The pressure in his suit drops from about forty percent of normal air pressure, down to about twenty-seven.
Every bit he lets out brings him closer to the bends, the same sickness that hits divers when they come up too fast.
Nitrogen bubbles in the blood.
Finally, the suit shrinks enough. He squeezes in, head first, the wrong way.
Then he has to fold himself around inside that narrow tube just to close the hatch behind him.
He made it. Exhausted. But he made it.
Every week, we put one of these stories under the X-ray. If you want the next one, subscribing is how you find it.
Because the next astronaut didn't face a vacuum. He faced something much more ordinary.
Water.

### DROWNING IN SPACE
July sixteenth, twenty thirteen. The International Space Station.
Italian astronaut Luca Parmitano is outside on a spacewalk. Just a week earlier, he became the first Italian ever to do one.
About forty-four minutes in, he reports something strange.
There's water. On the back of his head. Inside his helmet.
Nobody knows where it's coming from.
And here's what makes it terrifying.
On Earth, water in your helmet would fall to the bottom. In space, nothing falls.
Water clings. It spreads across surfaces. Across your skin.
It covers his ears. His radio cuts out. He can't hear his partner anymore.
It creeps over his eyes. Into his nose.
And it's moving toward his mouth.
He can't wipe it away. You can't open a helmet in space.
Mission Control calls off the spacewalk. Parmitano has to make his way back to the airlock, half blind and mostly deaf.
When the crew inside finally pulled off his helmet, they found between one and one and a half liters of water inside.
More than enough to drown in.
The suspected source? The suit's own cooling system. Water that runs through tubes in the underwear astronauts wear beneath the suit, to keep them from overheating.
A suit that can protect you from the vacuum of space... nearly drowned a man with a liter of water.
Which brings us to the last story. The one where nothing could save them.

### THE ONLY THREE
Out of everyone who has ever flown to space, only three people have died up there.
Not on a spacewalk. Inside their own capsule. Minutes from home.
June, nineteen seventy-one. Three Soviet cosmonauts, Georgy Dobrovolsky, Vladislav Volkov and Viktor Patsayev, are coming back from twenty-two days aboard Salyut One. The first space station in history.
They were the first crew ever to live on one.
They're flying without pressure suits. With three people aboard, there simply wasn't room for them.
On the way down, the capsule separates from the rest of the spacecraft.
And at that moment, a small valve jerks open.
It was meant to open only near the ground, about four kilometers up, to let fresh air in.
Instead, it's open in space. And the cabin air starts rushing out.
The valve is under a seat. Hard to reach. Impossible to close in time.
According to some accounts, Patsayev managed to get it partly closed before he passed out.
You already know what the next fifteen seconds feel like.
The capsule kept going. It came through the atmosphere, opened its parachute, and landed exactly as planned.
The recovery team ran up and knocked on the hatch.
Nobody answered.

### THE RULE
After Soyuz Eleven, everything changed.
Cosmonauts were required to wear pressure suits during the trip home. That rule is still in place today.
Every time you see astronauts launching in those bulky suits, you're seeing a lesson written by those three men.
So let's run it back one last time.
You don't explode. Your blood doesn't boil. You don't freeze into ice.
You swell. Your tongue fizzes. Your face goes numb.
And in about fifteen seconds, the lights go out.
So if you ever find yourself in an airlock, and the door starts to open...
breathe out.
Then start counting.
Fifteen seconds to do something smart.
About ninety for someone to save you.
